Abstract

Problem
A content team that can review fifty pages a week has to choose fifty out of tens of thousands, and FlyRank's own portfolio analysis shows why the choice is expensive: content peaks in measured health between 61 and 90 days, falls sharply past 270, and pages refreshed before that drop perform close to new content while untouched ones do not recover on their own.
Method
Features are built from one calendar month of Google Search Console performance and slow-changing content metadata; the label is measured in the month after, so no input can see its own outcome. A Random Forest is compared against a transparent hand-written rule on identical client-grouped folds, and every feature is audited for leakage before any score is reported.
Dataset
The pseudonymised FlyRank warehouse release, roughly 79 million rows of daily search performance. After the eligibility gate the study covers 93,654 pages across 38 client portfolios, with February 2026 as the feature window and March 2026 as the outcome window.
Result
Across five folds the model observed a mean Precision@50 of 0.836 against the rule's 0.680, and the queue it produces is reliable at the head: the fifty highest-priority pages carry roughly 699,000 February impressions between them. Under a naive random split the same model reports 1.000 with no variance at all, an overstatement of 0.164 on that metric.
Conclusion
The queue is a ranking tool, not an inventory of risk. Measured recall is about 6%, so it finds one declining page in sixteen, and the misses share a profile — pages that were growing before they turned — which a single-month feature window cannot detect by construction.
1

The problem this supports

FlyRank manages content portfolios across dozens of client brands. In its own March 2026 portfolio study the pattern is consistent: content reaches peak measured health between 61 and 90 days, holds through a maturation plateau, and then falls sharply in the 271–365 day band. Pages refreshed before that drop perform close to new content; pages left alone do not recover on their own.

That turns an editorial question into a scheduling one. The lever is timing and the constraint is attention: a reviewer has finite hours and the portfolio has far more pages than hours. Choosing the wrong fifty pages costs a month of decay on the right ones.

This work supports exactly that choice. It does not decide what to write, does not act on any page, and does not claim that refreshing a page recovers its traffic. It produces an ordered list with a reason attached to each row, so a person can start at the top on Monday and disagree with any line of it.

The decision, stated precisely

Given one month of measured search performance for every page in the portfolio, order those pages so that the ones most likely to lose impressions next month — weighted by how much traffic is at stake — appear first.

Data

All work runs on the pseudonymised FlyRank internship warehouse release, a Parquet dataset of roughly 79 million rows of daily search performance. Client and content identifiers are hashes; no names, domains, URLs, page titles or search queries exist in the release or appear anywhere in this paper.

Windows

Features come from February 2026. The label is measured over March 2026. The two are separated by the calendar rather than by a cut inside one month, so no feature can overlap the period it is predicting. The decision moment is 28 February 2026: every input is measurable on or before that date.

The most recent month in the release is deliberately left untouched. It is the natural outcome window for any past-to-future label, and developing label logic there would mean tuning against an answer key.

Eligibility, and what it removes

A page enters the frame if it earned at least 50 impressions in February. Below that, a change of a few impressions is noise rather than signal. This leaves 93,654 pages across 38 clients.

February contains 54 clients in the raw data. The eligibility gate removes 16 of them entirely, and coverage among the remaining 38 is very uneven — median 532 pages per client, minimum 2, maximum 20,358. This is recorded as a limitation rather than smoothed over.

Fields excluded, and why

  • Anything measured in March other than the label. A page's own outcome cannot be an input to predicting that outcome.
  • Update and optimisation dates. The content dimension is a current-state snapshot, not history: a page updated in March carries a March date, so these fields can encode the label window while looking like ordinary metadata. They are the most dangerous columns in the release because they would raise no suspicion in a feature list.
  • Position columns. Inspected day by day, the average-position field returns values below 1 — one page shows 3,519 impressions against a position sum of 524, giving 0.149. Position 1 is the best rank Google awards, so a value below 1 is not a real position. The column failed inspection and was dropped; no ranking-based claim appears in this paper as a result.
  • Analytics engagement columns. Only 4.2% of March rows carry analytics availability, so session, scroll and engagement signals would be missing for roughly 96% of the portfolio. A single availability flag is kept; the values are not.
  • Identifiers. Client and content hashes are used for grouping and joining only. As features they would let a model memorise portfolios instead of learning why pages decline.
3

Methodology

Label

A page is marked declining when its March daily impression rate falls below 80% of its February daily impression rate. February has 28 days and March has 31, so the comparison is made on daily rates; comparing raw totals would make almost every page look like it grew. This gives a base rate of 27.6%, or 25,887 declining pages.

This is a proxy for “this page needs a reviewer's attention”, not a record of whether anyone should have acted. No editorial outcome exists in the data.

Features

Twenty-three model-ready columns in four groups, all measurable on 28 February.

  • Volume. Impressions, clicks, and the count of days with at least one impression.
  • Rates. Click-through rate and impressions per active day. A page earning 1,000 impressions across three days behaves differently from one earning 1,000 across twenty-eight, and totals alone cannot tell them apart.
  • Momentum. Second-half February impressions divided by first half. Both halves sit inside the feature window, so the comparison is legal; pages with no first-half traffic are filled at 1.0 and flagged separately, because “collapsed to nothing” and “had not appeared yet” are opposite stories that would otherwise share a value.
  • Content context. Word count, age, search volume, competition, backlinks, plus content type and intent as encoded categories. Ordinal encoding where the levels have a natural order, one-hot where they do not.

Missing values are filled with a stated rule rather than dropped, and each fill is paired with a flag where the absence itself carries information. No rows are lost.

Baseline

A transparent hand-written rule scoring the same pages on the same label: 60% weight on momentum loss, 25% on inconsistent visibility, 15% on content age. It is readable in one line and needs no training, which is exactly what a model has to beat to justify itself.

Validation design

Five-fold cross-validation grouped by client. Every page of a client sits entirely in training or entirely in testing, never split across both.

This matters because pages from one client share a site, a topic mix and a traffic pattern. A random row split lets a model see some of a client's February pages with their March outcomes and then predict other pages from the same client in the same period — recognising portfolios rather than learning decline. Section 4 opens with what that is worth.

Leakage checks

Three checks plus a control, run on the final feature set. Correlation with the label peaks at 0.124. No single feature scores above 0.90 alone. Every feature's source window is asserted in code, so the notebook fails rather than printing a claim that has gone stale.

The control is the part that earns the others. Scoring March impressions alone — the label's own ingredient — returns 0.767, below the 0.90 screening threshold. A column that leaks the answer would have passed the single-feature check undetected, because the label is a ratio and March alone is ambiguous: 500 impressions is a collapse for a large page and growth for a small one. Single-feature screening is necessary but not sufficient here; the window audit is what catches such a column, because it tests provenance rather than predictive strength.

Results

The same model, two ways of testing it

One choice separates these numbers: whether a client's pages are allowed to appear in both training and testing. Switch between them.

Honest. Whole clients are held out, so the model is tested on portfolios it has never seen. The wide spreads are real uncertainty: the score depends on which clients land in the test fold.

A perfect Precision@50 with zero variance across five folds is a warning, not an achievement. It means the five folds were not really five experiments: each contained the same clients, so the model could recognise which portfolios had a bad March instead of learning why pages decline. The near-zero spread is the tell, and under the honest design that spread widens to ±0.142 — real uncertainty the random split was hiding rather than noise the grouped split introduced. Every headline number in this paper is a claim about a validation design as much as about a model.

A simple model loses to the rule

Logistic regression scores 0.592 ROC AUC against the rule's 0.633. That is a result rather than a failure: it says the relationship between these features and decline is not linear, and that three hand-chosen signals carry more together than a linear combination of twenty-three features does. Complexity alone bought nothing here; the model class was what mattered.

The forest leads, but not cleanly

Precision@50 rises from 0.680 to 0.836 and ROC AUC from 0.633 to 0.685. The spreads overlap, though: the rule ranges roughly 0.45 to 0.91 across folds and the forest 0.69 to 0.98, so in some client mixes the rule may have matched it. The forest is also the more stable of the two, at ±0.045 ROC AUC against the rule's ±0.085, which is a second reason to prefer it beyond the mean. This is a directional preference, not a demonstrated win.

What the model leans on

Permutation importance, measured as the drop in ROC AUC when a feature is shuffled, puts February momentum first at 0.0525 — roughly twice impressions per active day at 0.0233, with word count third at 0.0154. The ordering matches the single-feature screen, where momentum also scored highest alone.

This is the finding most likely to be read too quickly. Decline rises from 19.6% for pages under 90 days to 30.7% at 91–180 days — and then falls to 25.3% past a year. Older content does not simply decline more. A plausible reading is survivorship: a page still earning impressions after a year has already proven durable, while fragile old pages dropped below the eligibility gate long ago. That reading is untested here, and age alone cannot carry a refresh decision.

Where the model is wrong

On 20,358 held-out pages the model produced 300 true positives, 281 false positives and 4,651 false negatives.

The misses share a profile. Their median February momentum is 1.182, meaning they were growing through the feature window and then turned. Every signal the model holds said those pages were healthy. The true positives, by contrast, were already falling at 0.405 before the label window opened.

So the model is not weak at ranking. It is blind to a specific case: it finds pages that were already sliding and continued to slide, and cannot find pages that turn. That is a limitation of the one-month feature window, not of the model class.

5

Limitations and honest framing

Every claim in this paper is an association measured in one portfolio over two months. Nothing here demonstrates causation, and nothing here describes how Google ranks pages.

  • It ranks; it does not cover. Recall of roughly 6% means the queue is a priority order, not an inventory of at-risk content. Anyone treating the full list as a risk register would be wrong about most of it.
  • It cannot see pages that turn. A single-month feature window can only detect decline that had already started. Catching reversals would need several months of prior momentum, which the release supports and this design does not use.
  • Sixteen clients are absent entirely. The eligibility gate removes them, so the queue is silent about those portfolios rather than saying their pages are healthy.
  • Coverage is uneven. One client accounts for roughly 22% of the queue. A reviewer working the global ranking will spend most of their time on a handful of large portfolios; per-client coverage needs ranking within client.
  • One month predicts the next. Seasonality, algorithm updates and one-off events inside the window are invisible, and nothing in the design accounts for them.
  • The label is a proxy. An impression drop stands in for “needs attention”. No editorial outcome exists in the data, so the model cannot learn whether acting would have helped.
  • No ranking diagnosis. The position field failed inspection and was excluded, so this work cannot say a page slipped in the results.
  • Model and rule are close. The margin is directional. The hand-written rule remains a reasonable fallback, and losing the model would not be a crisis.
  • Refresh impact is not established. Showing that refreshing a page recovers traffic would need an experiment with a control group. This is an observational study and cannot provide one.
6

Ranked recommendations

The queue orders pages by decline probability weighted by the logarithm of February impressions. Weighting matters: ranking on probability alone put pages with 55 to 600 impressions and no clicks at the top, where the model was right that they would decline but nobody gains from fixing them. Using raw impressions instead would collapse the queue into a volume ranking. The log keeps risk as the driver and lets the size of the loss break ties.

  1. Start at the top and stop when the week ends

    The ranking is reliable at the head of the list and thin in the tail. Working the first fifty is the intended use; working the first five thousand is not.

  2. Read the columns, not the label

    Reason codes are evaluated in order, and momentum is checked first, so every page in the top twenty carries the same code even where age or click capture is also poor. The code is a triage hint; the underlying numbers are the evidence.

    Momentum loss · Intermittent visibility · Ageing content · Weak click capture · Model signal only
  3. Confirm the decline is real before calling it decay

    A steep drop inside one month is as consistent with deindexing, a redirect, a URL change or cannibalisation as it is with staleness. Pages losing more than three quarters of their impressions deserve a technical check before an editorial one.

  4. Treat the flagged pages differently

    Where the model and the hand-written rule disagree by more than forty percentile points — 22,004 pages, about 23.5% — the two methods are reading the page differently. Those rows need a judgment call rather than a queue position. None of them appear in the top twenty, where the two agree.

  5. Do not automate anything downstream of this

    No rewrites, no metadata changes, no deletion, no merging, no deindexing without a person reading the page first. At 6% recall the evidence does not support irreversible actions.

  6. Rebuild monthly and watch four numbers

    The feature and label windows are monthly, so the cadence matches the data, and peer medians and fills all derive from a single month's population. Rebuild rather than update. Monitor the base rate, the momentum distribution, the share of pages where model and rule disagree, and the number of clients passing the gate.

Reproducibility

Every number in this paper is produced by a notebook in the repository, and the run that produced each figure is recorded in a metrics file alongside it.

  • Repository — all notebooks and outputs
  • Data contract — grain, windows, availability
  • Feature vector and leakage hunt
  • Signal checks and baseline rule
  • Model and comparison
  • Validation audit — the split comparison in section 4
  • Action playbook — the queue and its limits

Environment: Python 3.12 with DuckDB, pandas and scikit-learn. The ranked queue itself is regenerated on every run and deliberately kept out of version control; figures and metrics files are committed, so any number quoted here can be traced to the run that produced it.
